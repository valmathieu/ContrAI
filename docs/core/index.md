# contrai-core

Shared domain model for the ContrAI workspace — pure data + invariants, no orchestration.

## Module map

Source lives at `packages/contrai-core/src/contrai_core/`:

| Module          | Contents                                                                                |

## Class structure


The full domain model in one view. `Trick` is a dumb container of plays that does **not** store trump — `Trick.get_current_winner(trump_suit)` takes trump as a *required* argument and works on partial tricks. Nothing in the engine builds one any more — the play phase is `PlayState` end to end, and the trick-winner rule is reached through `PlayState.trick_winners`, the partner-master legality check, and the view's live winner highlight, all of which call the shared `current_winner` function below. `Trick` remains a public core type with its own tests; whether it still earns its place is an open question. `Bid` and its four variants are now frozen `@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)` value carriers — player is `field(compare=False)` so equality is *what was bid, not who bid it*, and the auction-state rules ("is this bid legal now?") live entirely on `Auction`. `Auction.is_legal` / `legal_actions` / `apply` replace what used to be `Bid.is_valid_after` and the `BidValidator` utility — including the *auction-freezes-after-a-Double* rule from `contree-domain.md §5.3`. `Auction.apply` raises `IllegalBidError` rather than silently downgrading an illegal bid to a Pass. The defending team is computed at the game level, where both teams are in scope — `Contract` only knows its own attacking side. See [Diagrams](../diagrams/) for the colour convention.

**Seats own the geometry; `TeamSide` owns the identity.** `Position`'s definition order *is* the anticlockwise turn order, and every geometric question derives from that one ordering rather than re-encoding it: `next`, `partner`, `opponents`, `is_teammate` ("same side?"), and `team_side` ("which side?"). Turn direction is *threaded*, not baked in — the definition order stays the anticlockwise seating and `next_in(direction)` walks that same ordering backwards for a clockwise table, with `next` as its anticlockwise shorthand. `partner`, `opponents` and `team_side` take no direction: the partner is across the table and the opponents are the other two seats whichever way play runs. `TeamSide` (`NS` / `EW`) is the team identity — the key `RoundScore.scores`, `Round.round_scores`, `PlayState.card_points_by_side` / `trick_counts_by_side`, `Game.scores` and `GameOverStatus` all use, and the token a persisted game record or a training label would serialize. It replaces what used to be the free-form `Team.name` string: a *display* label doubling as a dict key, which meant renaming a label silently broke a lookup and every call site quietly leaned on `Team`'s identity semantics (it has no `__eq__`/`__hash__`). Like `Position`, `TeamSide` is a plain `Enum` and not a `StrEnum`, precisely so the old string keys stop comparing equal — a leftover `scores["North-South"]` must miss loudly rather than resolve by accident. `is_teammate` is expressed *on top of* `team_side` (`self.team_side is other.team_side`), so the boolean and the value can never disagree. What is left of `Team` is the two-player roster plus its display name; it carries no identity and no score, and the partner/membership helpers that used to re-derive the pairing from the roster list are gone.

`BasePlayer.position` is therefore *optional*: a player can exist before it has a seat. That is what lets the engine's `Game([p1, p2, p3, p4])` take an unseated roster and seat it in list order against `list(Position)` — which, because that order is the anticlockwise turn order, makes the first and third players partners rather than the first and second. Every caller used to have to pre-assign all four seats, which a simulation or training harness has no reason to care about. A pre-seated list keeps exactly the same validation, and a *half*-seated list is refused rather than completed: filling the gaps would silently decide partnerships the caller only specified half of. Randomised seating stays a caller-side `shuffle` of the list, which keeps the RNG and its seeding where a harness reseeding per game — or a test pinning a fixed table — can control it.

**Two suit types, one predicate.** `types.py` answers "what suit is this card?" and "what did the auction settle on?" with different types. `Suit` holds exactly the four card-bearing suits, so `tuple(Suit)` is safe to iterate anywhere a real suit is meant and `Card.__post_init__` can reject anything else outright (`InvalidCardError`). The two trump options that name no suit — `NO_TRUMP` and `ALL_TRUMP` — live on `TrumpVariant`, and `ContractSuit = Suit | TrumpVariant` is what a contract's trump is annotated as. It is a plain union assignment rather than a PEP 695 `type` alias on purpose: `isinstance(x, ContractSuit)` works on the plain form and raises `TypeError` on the alias form, and with no type checker configured in this workspace, `isinstance` narrowing is the only guardrail there is.

The two types meet in exactly one place, `is_trump(card_suit, contract_suit)`, plus its enumerating sibling `trump_suits(contract_suit)` and the `Card.is_trump(trump_suit)` sugar. Before that, the question was written out as `card.suit == trump_suit` at eleven sites in core and roughly as many again in the engine — a comparison that is only *accidentally* right for a trump naming no suit. It happens to answer correctly for `NO_TRUMP` (no card carries that suit, so every trump branch collapses to plain follow-suit, which is what a no-trump contract wants) and it answered the same way for `ALL_TRUMP`, where every card is trump instead — so an all-trump contract played out as a no-trump one, silently. Routing every caller through the `TrumpRules` seam is what gives the two variants their opposite answers: `is_trump(suit, ALL_TRUMP)` is `True` for all four suits and `trump_suits(ALL_TRUMP)` is the whole of `Suit`. One decision, taken once, instead of a coincidence repeated at each site.

**One rules object per contract regime.** `rules.py` is where every trick-taking decision that depends on what the auction settled on gets answered: which cards are trump, what each card scores, how cards rank inside a suit, who takes a trick, where a Belote can live. `rules_for(contract_suit)` resolves a `TrumpRules` object once per decision site — `SingleSuitRules(suit)` for a suit contract, `NoTrumpRules` for no-trump, `AllTrumpRules` for all trump — and callers hold it instead of re-deriving trumpness card by card. That is what made all trump a new leaf class plus its tables rather than a sweep over every call site that spells out a trump comparison: nothing in `PlayState.legal_actions`, `current_winner` or the scorer had to change to gain the regime.

All trump ranks every suit on the trump ladder, but it does **not** score on the trump table: 20/14/11/10 applied to four suits would put 62 points in a suit and 248 in the deck. The scale is compressed instead — Jack 14, 9 nine, Ace 6, 10 five, King 3, Queen 1 — so a suit is worth 38 and the deck 152, the same total as every other regime (`contree-domain.md` §3.3, §3.5). The *ordering* is untouched; only the valuation is. And what actually makes the regime distinct is `trick_rank`: with every card trump, "any trump beats any led-suit card" would be vacuous, so `AllTrumpRules.trick_rank` answers `None` for every suit but the led one. That single answer is what forbids cross-suit cutting (§6.4) — and, because `legal_actions` asks the seam rather than the contract, what puts a seat holding the led suit on the follow-*and*-raise branch and a void seat on the free-discard branch, with no new code.

Four design invariants hold it together. **No global "order" scale**: the trump and plain orderings are two overlapping 0–7 scales, so a single `order(card)` number would compare cards across scales and be silently wrong — same-suit comparisons go through `rank_in_suit`, and cross-suit trick competition through `trick_rank`, which is led-suit-aware and totally ordered by construction (`(1, trump_rank)` for trump, `(0, plain_rank)` for a led-suit card, `None` for a card that cannot take the trick). All trump is what proves the distinction load-bearing rather than pedantic: the over-trump obligation used to pick the best card on the table with `is_trump` plus `rank_in_suit`, which under all trump admits an off-suit *discard* and then ranks it on the trump scale — raising the bar with a card that never competed for the trick and legalising cards the seat may not play. It ranks through `trick_rank` now, the one comparator that knows what competes. **Obligations stay out**: follow/trump/over-trump structure lives in `PlayState.legal_actions`; rules objects only supply the predicates it asks with. **The hierarchy is sealed** — `__init_subclass__` rejects any subclass defined outside the module, because exhaustive `isinstance` narrowing over the known leaves is this workspace's only type guardrail. **Instances are singletons**: rules objects are stateless values, so `rules_for` hands out one shared instance per regime, identity checks stay meaningful, and the hot path allocates nothing.

**Three card tables, each totalling 152.** A suit contract scores trump on its own scale (Jack 20, 9 14) and every other suit on the plain scale (Ace 11, Ten 10). No-trump has no trump suit to carry the higher values, so reusing the plain table would leave a suit at 30 points and the deck at 120 — which is why no trump carries a *third*, rescaled table with the ace at 19, putting a suit at 38 and the deck at 152, the same total as a suit contract (`contree-domain.md` §3.4, §3.5). That constant total is what makes a contract value mean the same thing in either mode: a 130 contract is reachable at no trump precisely because the deck still holds 152. Ranking is *not* rescaled — no trump has always used the plain A-10-K-Q-J-9-8-7 ladder and still does, so only `points` differs between the two regimes. `test_rules.py` pins all three tables as literals (so a slip in the source cannot silently agree with itself) and asserts the 152 invariant per regime.

Because the seam owns the tables, **`Card` is pure identity**. The old call-time API — `Card.is_trump(trump_suit)`, `Card.get_points(trump_suit)`, `Card.get_order(trump_suit)` and the four class-level tables — is gone: a card asked to score itself had to be handed the contract's trump at every call site, which is the same coupling the seam exists to remove, and it left the tables sitting on the value object where a second regime could not add a third one. The module-level `is_trump` / `trump_suits` predicates remain as thin delegates over the seam.

**One config object for every table rule.** `rule_config.py` turns the variants catalogue of `contree-domain.md` §9 into a single frozen value: `RuleConfig` has one field per *configurable* row — 22 of them — named after the catalogue's own wording, so the doc and the dataclass can be read side by side. `RuleConfig()` *is* the §9 default set, and the three enums that type the non-boolean knobs (`TurnDirection`, `AllTrumpBelote`, `Rounding`) render as their config-file token (`str(Rounding.EXACT) == "exact"`), so one value spells the member in a TOML file, in a log line and in an error message alike. It lives in core rather than the engine because its future consumers do: card-play legality is `PlayState.legal_actions`, the trump regimes are `rules.py`, and the scoring components are the round scorer — every one of them a rule of the game, not a rule of the interface. Core stays I/O-free, so *loading* a ruleset is the engine's job (`contrai_engine.ruleset`); core only defines what a ruleset is and what makes one impossible.

Frozen plus `slots=True` makes a ruleset hashable, which is the point: a simulation result, a training run or a scraped game can name the ruleset it was played under, and two runs can be compared for identity rather than by re-deriving the knobs. Construction-time validation rejects exactly the two configurations §9 calls impossible — both marking conventions off (a table marking neither made nor announced points keeps no score at all) and a `target_score` off the 500–5000 ladder — raising `InvalidRuleConfigError`. The two combinations §9 records as *inert* are deliberately **not** errors: `failed_slam_marks_announced_points` has no effect unless `any_failure_marks_160` is on, and `all_trump_belote` is meaningless while `extended_trump_choices` is off. Rejecting them would refuse a config that is merely redundant, and a table setting a knob it will not reach is not making a mistake. Presets are core constructors: `RuleConfig.classic()` is the defaults *by name* — so a log can say which defaults, since defaults may move — and `PRESETS` is the registry the engine's `--preset` flag selects from. `belote-rebelote` (site parity for the scraper) is deferred until its values are actually observed.

**No knob changes behaviour yet.** The fields exist, are validated, are threaded down to `PlayState` and the round scorer, and are consulted by nothing. That is the deliberate shape of this step: the backbone is provable — every existing test stays green — and each knob gets wired to behaviour on its own, one step at a time.

`TrickRecord` is the completed-trick value the seam produces: an immutable four-record tuple that knows its own `led_suit` and answers `winner(trump_suit)` with the winning play *record* — a `Play` in state contexts, a sealed `ObservedPlay` in observations — never just the seat. It subclasses `tuple`, so every consumer that iterates, unpacks or compares a completed trick as a plain sequence keeps working unchanged; what it adds is that a completed trick can no longer be confused with a partial one, and that "who won this trick" is asked of the trick rather than recomputed by the caller.

**Two layers of bid validity.** A bid is checked twice, by two things that answer different questions. `ContractBid.__post_init__` answers *is this a well-formed bid at all* — a step on the 80–240 ladder naming one of the six contract trumps — and nothing more. `Auction` answers *is it legal at this table, right now*, and that is where the ruleset lands: `Auction` carries a frozen `rules: RuleConfig` (mirroring `PlayState.rules`), and `_is_contract_legal` consults two functions before it ever looks at auction precedence. `bookable_suits(rules)` is the trump-choice gate — the four card suits, plus `NO_TRUMP` and `ALL_TRUMP` only when `extended_trump_choices` is on, which the §9 default set leaves off. `ladder_top(contract_suit, rules)` is the per-mode numeric ceiling:

| Trump choice | Ladder top | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a suit | 180 | 162 on cards and the last-trick bonus, plus one Belote |
| no trump | 160 | no Belote exists at no trump |
| all trump, `none` | 160 | the regime awards no Belote |
| all trump, `single` | 180 | one Belote marks, whoever announced first |
| all trump, `four` | 240 | up to four pairs, +80 |

The split is what keeps the view's "build the bid, then ask whether it is legal" flow intact: the CLI's `_parse_bid_input` never raises on a bid the table forbids, it returns the bid and lets `Auction.is_legal` reject it with an explanation. The two functions live in `bid.py` rather than on `Auction` so the view can mirror the rules for its messages without reimplementing them. `SlamLevel` bids are deliberately **not** ladder-capped — §5.2 puts the Slam family under every trump choice at its own base value — which is why every cap comparison guards on `isinstance(value, int)` first: a `SlamLevel` has no ordering against an int at all.

**The three §9.4 switches are auction rules, not bid well-formedness.** `bookable_values(rules)` is the value-side sibling of `bookable_suits`: it returns `ContractBid.VALID_VALUES` in order, minus `SlamLevel.SOLO_SLAM` when the table has `solo_slam_available` off. Only the Solo Slam is a table's to withdraw — the numeric steps are *capped* per mode by `ladder_top`, never removed, and the plain Slam is available everywhere. Preserving the order matters: `legal_actions` probes precedence monotonically up `VALID_VALUES` and would break on a resorted list. A `ContractBid` for a withdrawn Solo Slam still **constructs** — it is a well-formed bid — and `Auction.is_legal` is the authority that refuses it, which is why the check sits in `_is_contract_legal` beside the trump-choice limit rather than in `__post_init__`: both are table rules no auction state can lift, and both must stay reversible by handing the auction a different `RuleConfig`.

Doubling is gated the same way, per contract, by `_slam_double_allowed`: `slam_can_be_doubled` and `solo_slam_can_be_doubled` are two independent switches, both on by default, and turning one off forbids the double against that contract alone. A numeric contract is never gated. The redouble needs no switch of its own — it only ever stands on a live double, so removing the double removes the redouble by construction.

One thing the auction deliberately does *not* check: that a bidder past 160 actually holds the Belote the step depends on. The auction never looks at hands. Bidding 240 at all trump without four pairs is legal and simply commits the bidder to a contract the cards alone cannot make.

`play.py` adds the play phase's sibling to `Auction`: `PlayState` is an immutable frozen dataclass (`slots=True`) that owns the flat chronological tuple of `Play` records — `Play` is a `NamedTuple` of `(player, card)`, unpacking exactly like the tuples `Trick` and the winner rule already consume. Like `Auction`, the bare constructor performs no validation so tests and search forks can inject arbitrary mid-round states directly; `PlayState.start(contract, players, hands)` is the validated entry point for a fresh deal (exactly 4 players, 4 eight-card hands, 32 distinct cards). Every other view — `trick_number`, `current_trick`, `completed_tricks`, `trick_winners`, `card_points_by_side`, `trick_counts_by_side`, `to_act`, `is_terminal()` — is recomputed from `plays` on each access rather than cached; `slots=True` forbids stashing lazy state, keeping the play history the single source of truth. The last two answer "what has each side captured so far": every completed trick's pile and the trick itself are credited to the side of the seat that won it, scored through the contract's `TrumpRules`, with every `TeamSide` present as a key so callers index rather than `.get`. They carry the **raw pile only** — the +10 last-trick and +20 Belote bonuses are contract-conversion rules that belong to whoever scores the round, which is why a completed round sums to the deck's 152 and not 162. One derivation now serves the engine's scorer and both of its screens, where each used to re-sum a pile of its own. `legal_actions(player)` is the player-parametric legality oracle: it enforces the follow/trump obligations (follow suit; over-trump when trump is led; trump if void and the partner is not currently master, over-trumping an opponent's ruff if able; free discard once the partner is master, or once no obligation applies at all — no trump suit, or none held) but deliberately **no turn check** — the same idiom `Auction.legal_actions` uses for bids, so a caller can ask "what would be legal for this player" independent of whose turn it is. `apply(play)` is the single legality-enforcing transition: it checks turn order, then hand membership, then the obligations, raising `IllegalPlayError` with the matching `PlayRuleViolation` — `OUT_OF_TURN` (wrong player, or the phase is already over), `CARD_NOT_IN_HAND`, or one of `MUST_FOLLOW_SUIT` / `MUST_TRUMP` / `MUST_OVERTRUMP` — plus the legal alternatives for diagnostics. `with_hands(hands)` forks the same public history (contract, players, plays) onto replacement per-seat hands: the determinization primitive a future search-based AI would sample worlds from — no search runs on it yet, this is only the fork primitive. The state also carries the table `RuleConfig` it is played under — `start(contract, players, hands, rules=…)` records it, `apply` and `with_hands` propagate it (so every determinized world is played under the same rules as the real one), and it is part of value equality; nothing reads a knob of it yet.

`observe(player, bids=...)` is `PlayState`'s sanctioned trust boundary: it projects the omniscient state — which holds every seat's hand — down to a `PlayObservation` carrying only `player`'s own hand, the public trick history, the established contract, the auction history the caller passes in, and `player`'s legal cards right now. This is the input surface AI card-play strategies are meant to be handed, never the raw `PlayState`. **Everything is sealed on the way out**: every person the observation would otherwise name arrives as a bare `Position`, the opaque seat enum. The observer is `observation.position`, not a player; the trick history carries `ObservedPlay` `(position, card)` records rather than the state's own live-player `Play` records; the contract is an `ObservedContract` (`declarer`, `value`, `suit`, `doubled_by`, `redoubled_by`) rather than the live `Contract`; and each bid is reseated by `seal_bid` into a `Bid[Position]`, keeping its concrete variant and payload. The guarantee that falls out is a reachability one — *no* object path from an observation reaches a `BasePlayer` — and it has to be that strong, because a single surviving reference exposes `.hand` directly and, through `.team.players`, the partner's hand as well. `test_play_observation.py` asserts it by walking the whole object graph rather than by checking fields one at a time. `observe()` still takes the live player: the state needs that identity to look up the hand and the legal actions, so only the output is sealed. Trick-winner determination for both `Trick` and `PlayState` shares one implementation, the module-level `current_winner(plays, trump_suit)` function in `trick.py` — generic over the "who" slot of its plays, so it serves `(BasePlayer, Card)` and sealed `(Position, Card)` records alike: `Trick.get_current_winner` delegates to it, and `PlayState.trick_winners` / `PlayObservation.current_winner` (which therefore reports a `Position`) call it directly — `to_act` reaches the same rule indirectly, via `trick_winners` — one winner rule, not two that could drift apart.

**Exception hierarchy.** Every domain error now subclasses a single `ContraiError` base, so one `except ContraiError` catches the whole family. Each concrete error *also* subclasses `ValueError` (dual inheritance, `ValueError` kept in the MRO) so legacy `except ValueError` call sites keep working unchanged. `IllegalPlayError` is the card-play counterpart to `IllegalBidError`: it carries the offending `Card`, a machine-readable `PlayRuleViolation` reason (`MUST_FOLLOW_SUIT` / `MUST_TRUMP` / `MUST_OVERTRUMP` / `OUT_OF_TURN` / `CARD_NOT_IN_HAND`, a `StrEnum` for clean logging/JSON), and the set of legal alternatives — serving the §6.1 explainability goal and future RL/scraper/server consumers. `TrickStateError` (adding to a complete trick) and `InvalidContractError` (bad contract value/suit, or a redouble without an underlying double) replace the last bare `ValueError`s raised by `Trick`, `ContractBid`, and `Contract`. `InvalidRuleConfigError` joins them for the table-rule catalogue's two construction-time checks.

## Consumers

- **`contrai-engine`** — direct dependency. Imports core types with `from contrai_core import …` and adds `Player` / `HumanPlayer` / `AiPlayer` / `Game` / `Round` on top.
- **`contrai-scraper`** — planned consumer. Observed games will be materialized into `Card` / `Bid` / `Trick` / … instances before being persisted to SQLite.
- **`contrai-analyzer`** — **does not** depend on core. The analyzer's `SuitSlot` (TRUMP/BLUE/GREEN/PURPLE) is a suit-agnostic abstraction for probability math, intentionally separate from `Suit` — it slots a hand by *role* relative to trump, where `Suit` names the four concrete suits. See the [analyzer overview](../analyzer/index.md).

## Conventions

- Type hints everywhere, including private helpers.
- Google-style docstrings on every public class/method/function.
- Didactic comments are welcome — this is a learning project.
- Every Model-layer addition ships with `pytest` tests under `packages/contrai-core/tests/`.

## Tests

Coverage is now complete across every module:
`test_types.py`, `test_position.py`, `test_team_side.py`, `test_card.py`, `test_deck.py`, `test_hand.py`, `test_card_queries.py`, `test_rules.py`, `test_rule_config.py`, `test_team.py`, `test_base_player.py`, `test_bid.py`, `test_auction.py`, `test_contract.py`, `test_trick.py`, `test_play_state.py`, `test_play_legality.py`, `test_play_observation.py`, `test_exceptions.py`.

`test_bid.py` covers the data contract of the frozen variants (construction validation, equality, ordering, `__str__`, immutability). The auction-state rules that used to be tested against `Bid.is_valid_after` and `BidValidator` now live in `test_auction.py` against `Auction.is_legal`, `legal_actions`, and `apply`. `test_play_state.py` covers `PlayState.start`'s validation, `apply`'s turn-order/hand-membership/immutability guarantees, `with_hands`'s determinization-fork validation, and the derived views' boundaries (including the no-trump degrade). `test_play_legality.py` exercises `legal_actions` against the follow/over-trump/trump obligation matrix and `apply`'s resulting `PlayRuleViolation` classification. `test_play_observation.py` covers `observe`'s own-hand-only projection, its legal-cards parity with `PlayState.legal_actions`, bids pass-through, the derived properties, immutability, the sealed trick records and sealed contract — and, in `TestNothingLiveIsReachable`, the seal itself stated as a property: a structural walk over the whole object graph of a fully populated observation (doubled contract, four-seat auction, a completed trick and a partial one) asserting that no `BasePlayer`, `Team` or `Hand` is reachable by any path, and that no `Card` outside the observer's own hand and the public history turns up. Two negative controls keep that walk honest — one confirms it finds the leaks in a live `Contract`, the other plants an unsealed `ContractBid` in an otherwise-sealed observation and confirms the walk reaches the player through it (a walk reading only `type(obj).__slots__` would miss it, since `ContractBid` inherits its `player` slot from `Bid`). `test_exceptions.py` covers the dual-inheritance invariant (every domain error is a subclass of both `ContraiError` and `ValueError`), the `PlayRuleViolation` `StrEnum`, and the message/attribute contract of each error; the construction-validation tests in `test_bid.py` / `test_contract.py` / `test_trick.py` assert the specific new types. The remaining engine-side gap is `Round` — see [`engine/index.md`](../engine/index.md#open-work).
